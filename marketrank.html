MarketRank · Fluxscape

Where does the market's money settle?

Every trading day money leaves some stocks and moves into others. MarketRank models that as a Markov chain over stocks and ranks each one by the share of money that settles on it in the long run.

Read the paper (PDF) View on GitHub Run it yourself

The idea in one equation

Stocks are the states of a Markov chain. The chance of moving from stock j to stock i is the share of j's outgoing dollars that goes to i. A stock's MarketRank is its stationary probability: where a dollar ends up after the flows have run for a long time.

ri = (1 − p) · Σj rj · Tj→i / Σk Tj→k + p / N, p = 0.15

This is the same construction as PageRank, with dollars in place of hyperlinks. A stock ranks high when it receives a large share of what its senders give away, and when those senders themselves rank high. The rank is a property of the whole flow network, not of a stock's own trading volume. The score is shown as π·N, so 1 means an average stock.

From public prices to a flow graph

Trades are anonymous: the tape shows that a stock traded, not which stock the buyer sold to fund it. MarketRank therefore estimates the flows from public daily bars. Each stock's signed dollar pressure is paired, gravity-style, with the stocks that moved the other way, tilted toward the stocks it usually moves with. The result is a sparse transition matrix over 10,000 US stocks and ETFs, solved by damped power iteration in a deterministic parallel C++ engine.

The interface

The exact ranked table is always the reference. Around it, Fluxscape turns the chain into views you can read:

  • Landscape. Stocks are grouped into territories by the communities of the recent flow graph. The height and colour show π relative to size: red attracts more money than its size predicts, blue less.
  • Sections (the default). The landscape is seen from above with a crosshair, and two profile charts cut through it, plotting every stock on the cut at its exact value.
  • Paths. Any stock can be tracked over the whole history. You drag the chart to move through time, and several stocks can share one chart.
  • The transition graph. A close-up of the chain itself around the tracked stocks. Each arrow carries the transition probability through which one stock passes its rank to another.

What we found, honestly

The practical question is whether MarketRank can improve real portfolio moves. We fixed the tests in advance and report them as they came out.

TestResult
Ten-year weekly walk-forward (2016–2026), after costs, against simply holding the portfolioNot yet profitable: every configuration trailed buy-and-hold by 3–5% a year. One persistent effect, a short-term reversal in which today's hottest names do slightly worse, is real but small.
Estimated flows against observed institutional flows (SEC Form 13F, 39 quarters, 4,000–9,000 managers each)The estimate gets roughly right which stocks send and receive money, and a little about when. It does not yet capture who pairs with whom.
How long do flow communities persist?About as long as the flow window that defines them: half-life about a week on 20-day flows.
Can the connectivity be learned? Three tests registered in advance: weekly, a correction to the chain at 15 minutes, and the chain as it is on two years of real 15-minute data for the 1,000 most liquid namesNo. On synthetic markets, neither learned model recovers a planted graph at realistic signal levels, so neither was run on real data. On real 15-minute data the chain predicts the next 1.5 hours no better than graph-free controls, and no better than the same chain attached to the wrong stocks.

The solver is sound. Its transition probabilities are only as good as the connectivity fed into it, and public bars carry size and a little timing, not pairing, at daily and intraday horizons alike.

What's next: data that observe pairing

The transition-probability graph stays at the core. Learning its connectivity from public bars did not work, weekly or at 15 minutes, so the next step is data that see pairing more directly: ETF creation and redemption, signed order flow classified trade by trade, and observed 13F pairs mixed into the chain. Each will be re-tested with the same walk-forward harness, the same 13F nulls, and a control that attaches the chain to the wrong stocks.

Run it yourself

The code is open source on GitHub: github.com/karametebkaan/marketrank. It builds on Linux x86-64 with CMake ≥ 3.24, a C++20 compiler, and the OpenSSL and OpenMP development packages (Ubuntu: sudo apt install build-essential cmake libssl-dev libomp-dev). CMake downloads the rest, DuckDB included.

git clone https://github.com/karametebkaan/marketrank && cd marketrank
cmake -S . -B build -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release && cmake --build build -j
# demo market, no keys needed; open http://127.0.0.1:8765
./build/marketrank --serve --mode synthetic

For real US stocks and ETFs, add free Alpaca market-data keys to .env (copy .env.example), download the daily bars once, then open the interface over the cached data:

# download the daily bars and solve (once)
./build/marketrank --mode alpaca --universe-size 10000
# the interface shown above, on http://127.0.0.1:8765
./build/marketrank --serve --mode replay

The README covers every option: rank tables, counterfactual shocks, the walk-forward evaluation and the 13F comparison.
